# Rome: nets, nooses, grapnels and boarding bridges (research, 2026-10-24)

How to read the status tags:
- **Solid**: a primary text I read, or a consistent reading across several secondary sources.
- **Debated**: scholars disagree, or the claim rests on secondary sources I did not check against the text.
- **Unverified**: I could not confirm it. Excluded from the game section unless marked.

Conversions are mine: Roman cubit about 44 cm, Roman foot about 29.6 cm.

---

## 1. Laquearius (noose gladiator)

- **Latin**: *laquearius*, also *laquerarius* and *laqueator* (from *laqueus*, "noose, snare").
- **Primary text (solid)**: Isidore, *Etymologies* 18.56 (LVI, "De laqueariis"). I read the Latin. It says the laquearii fought by throwing a noose over men fleeing in the arena, tangling them, then chasing them down and knocking them over with the shield boss (*umbo*). Source: https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/isidore/18.shtml
- **Dictionary**: Lewis and Short gloss *laqueator* as an "ensnarer," a gladiator who tried to catch an opponent with a noose, citing Isidore 18.56 (some manuscripts read *laquearius*). The entry for *laquearius* itself gives "ceiling-maker" as the primary sense and says "perhaps" for the gladiator sense. Source: https://cld.bbaw.de/lemma/lat/laqueator
- **Modern summary (secondary)**: the Wikipedia article gives a noose in one hand and a dagger or sword in the other, and calls the class late. Its citations are only to Tegg, Cowper, Junkelmann and Baker by page, with no section numbers. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laquearius
- **Debated**: whether it was a full bout class or a comic type. The article suggests the paegniarius (mock fighter) as an alternative and says a Roman lasso model is unlikely because Romans did not use lassos in battle. A museum record (seen only in a search snippet, unverified) says a mould once labelled laquearius is more likely a paegniarius.
- **Weights and lengths**: none in the sources.

## 2. Retiarius (net and trident)

- **Primary text**: none for the cord. The evidence is iconography: mosaics, reliefs, graffiti, shoulder guards. The Wikipedia article is the best summary I found. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retiarius
- **Cord to wrist (solid, with a caveat)**: the article says a rope runs round the net's edge with its ends tied to the fighter's wrist. A drawcord tightened the net after a hit, to topple the opponent. A miss let him haul the net back. If the secutor grabbed the net, the retiarius could cut the cord with his dagger. Tribality and Historyrise say the same. Imperium Romanum cites a brick image (Equoreus) with the net tied to the left wrist. I did not check that object.
- **Debated**: the article calls the frequency of net use "speculation" and says few images show nets. Pulling back by drawcord is an inference from the same sources.
- **Dimensions**: net about 3 m across with lead weights round the edge, from modern reconstructions and fishing-net comparisons (Wikipedia calls these "the only clues"). Trident iron or bronze, about person height, no weight given. Total kit 7 to 8 kg, the lightest of the standard types.

## 3. Harpax / harpago (Agrippa at Naulochus, 36 BC)

- **Primary (solid)**: Appian, *Civil Wars* 5.118-119. I read Thayer's White translation. The device is a grip (the translator's word, not harpax). It is a timber about five cubits long (about 2.2 m), bound with iron, with a ring at each end. An iron claw is on one ring and many ropes on the other. It was thrown by catapult onto enemy ships, and machine power (a winch) drew the ship in once the claw caught. Section 5.119 says the light weight let it be thrown a long way, and it grabbed ships when the ropes pulled from behind. Source: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/5*.html
- **Greek names**: *harpax*, *harpago*, *harpaga*, a grappling tool, in the Liddell-Scott lexicon and in 19th-century dictionaries (Smith, Harper). Those sources describe a household flesh-hook first.
- **Battle figures (secondary)**: Wikipedia gives Sextus losing 180 of 300 warships, 28 rammed and 155 captured or burned, citing Murray et al. (2007). Note that 28 + 155 = 183, not 180. I did not check the primary figures. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpax
- **Debated**: whether Agrippa invented it. The Wikipedia article says an earlier harpago was "said to have been invented by Pericles" with no citation. I dropped that claim.
- **Dropped**: the reuse at Actium in 31 BC (only a blog). The Wikipedia claim that the ropes could not be cut does not appear in the Appian passage as far as I read it. My summary of the text said the iron bands were hard to cut, so check the Greek before using either.
- **Weights**: none given. Length is the five cubits above.

## 4. Corvus (boarding bridge, Duilius, 260 BC)

Not a hook, but the closest Roman grapple-type device to a boarding bridge.

- **Latin**: *corvus* ("crow"). Used first at Mylae (260 BC, consul Duilius), per Polybius 1.22. Also Ecnomus (Polybius 1.25-1.29).
- **Dimensions (solid as Polybius's figures, quoted by secondary sources)**: a bridge 36 ft (about 10.9 m) long and 4 ft (about 1.2 m) wide, with a low parapet at each side. A pole 24 ft (about 7.3 m) high at the bow, with a pivot and pulley at the top. An iron spike at the far end pinned the bridge to the enemy deck. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvus_(boarding_device)
- **Weight**: about one ton, from J. W. Bonebakker's estimate (secondary).
- **Debated (solid as a debate)**: Wallinga (1956) argued the weight made ships unstable. Bonebakker disagreed. Tarn argued it never existed as described and may have been an enhanced grapnel pole. Steinby questions how decisive it was. A Heidelberg article argues it is largely fiction (search snippet only).
- **Dropped**: the Latin name *manus ferrea* ("iron hand"). No source I read supports it as a name for the corvus. Wikipedia's Harpax article uses *manus ferreae* as an uncited comparison.

## 5. Lupus, falx muralis and ram-catching

- **Unverified.** I could not reach the Latin of Vegetius, *Epitoma* Book 4. The thelatinlibrary URL returned 404, and the search did not return the passage.
- The Roman siege engines article does not mention a *lupus*, a *falx muralis*, or iron hooks for seizing rams. Its Vegetius citations concern the *testudo* and stones for ballistae.
- The only lead is a Latin lexicon note that *lupus* is also the name of a military engine, with its nature unknown. A medieval "war-wolf" (Stirling 1304) is later and not Roman, so I excluded it.
- **Suggested check**: the Onnerfors Teubner text (1995) or Milner's translation (Liverpool, 1996), Epitoma 4 siegecraft chapters.

## 6. Steppe and Persian lassos

- **Pausanias 1.21.5 (solid)**: the Sauromatae (Sarmatians) throw a lasso round any enemy they meet, then turn their horses so the snared enemy is upset. This is a primary text, read in translation. Source: https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias1B.html
- **Herodotus 7.85 (fairly solid)**: the Sagartians, nomads in Xerxes' army, carried no iron or bronze except daggers and used ropes of plaited thongs with nooses. Riders snared enemy horsemen or foot soldiers and pulled them in. I did not fetch the Greek. Godley and Macaulay agree on the nooses. Source (search result): https://lexundria.com/hdt/7.85/mcly
- **Pomponius Mela 1.114 and Josephus BJ 7.7.4 (Alans)**: cited for lassos in Istvánovits (2001) and the Suda. I did not read the Latin or Greek. The English translation of BJ 7 I could see does not mention a lasso. **Debated**.
- **Dropped**: Ammianus (no lasso found). The Persian *kamand* as a term (no ancient source found). A Roman army lasso (none found). A Kazakhstan site claim that Sarmatians tried to take an Armenian king with a lasso, which conflicts with the Alan attribution.

## 7. Other hooks and grapnels

- *Harpago* as a household flesh-hook and a cooking tool (19th-century dictionaries via Wikipedia). Solid as a dictionary claim only.
- *Harpagones* (Roman grapnels): mentioned in passing by Wikipedia, uncited. Unverified beyond that.
- No bola or other rope-throwing weapon from Roman Italy was researched in depth.

## Game mechanics (suggested)

**Laquearius: noose throw.** A ranged tether aimed at a fleeing target. On a hit the target is snared (cannot sprint or dodge out for a few seconds), and the attacker closes in to knock them down with the shield boss, as Isidore describes. *Counter*: cut the cord with a blade (a single slash, or a rope health bar), or step into the thrower to close distance before the loop tightens.

**Retiarius: net cast with drawcord.** A short-range net (about 3 m in game scale, weighted edge). A hit entangles the target: movement is cut and a stagger applies. The attacker can haul the drawcord to topple them. *Counter*: dodge out of the cast arc before release. Or, once the net lands, grab it and cut the cord with a dagger (Wikipedia and Tribality both describe this).

**Harpax: catapult grapnel on a winch.** Shoot a claw at an enemy ship or siege engine. If it catches, the ropes and a crew winch pull the target toward the thrower, as in Appian 5.118-119. Useful for hauling a siege engine or ship into boarding range. *Counter*: crew cut the ropes, or move beyond the rope length before the winch engages. The counter-pull is a design choice, not a source fact.

**Corvus: boarding bridge drop.** A deck-mounted bridge (36 ft by 4 ft) drops onto an enemy deck and pins with a spike, so the attacker can board. Its weight (about a ton, debated) makes the deploying ship slow and unstable. *Counter*: stay out of the drop arc, cut the pivot rope, or have a crew member lift the spike off the deck.

**Steppe lasso: loop and turn.** A mounted attacker throws a loop, then turns the horse to upset the snared enemy (Pausanias 1.21.5). *Counter*: cut the loop, sidestep before the cast, or block with a shield.

**Lupus and falx muralis**: not included. Unverified.
